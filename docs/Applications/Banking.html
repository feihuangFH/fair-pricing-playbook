Application: Banking and Lending

Responsible AI for Algorithmic Pricing

Author

Fei Huang, UNSW Sydney

TipHow to use this page

This page shows how the four-step playbook applies to consumer lending and credit. The steps themselves are unchanged. This page translates the framing, terminology, and regulatory context for banking practitioners.

Read the four steps for the technical depth. This page bridges the concepts to the lending context.

Why lending raises the same fairness questions

Algorithmic credit pricing shares the structural problem that motivates this playbook. A model trained on repayment history, income, and geography can produce discriminatory outcomes even after explicitly protected attributes such as race are excluded. Research on US mortgage and auto lending consistently finds that minority borrowers face higher rates or lower approval odds than comparable white borrowers, even when the lender uses FinTech models that do not observe race directly (Bartlett et al. 2022; Fuster et al. 2022).

The mechanism is the same as in insurance. Race correlates with zip code, which correlates with the lending algorithm’s inputs. The model never observes race, but it approximates it through proxies. Removing race from the feature set is not sufficient. It is a starting point.

Regulatory landscape

Jurisdiction Key rule What it requires
United States Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA, 15 U.S.C. §1691) Prohibits discrimination in credit on race, colour, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, or public assistance status. Covers approvals, pricing, and terms (U.S. Congress 1974)
United States CFPB fair lending supervision Monitors both disparate treatment and disparate impact. SR 11-7 (Federal Reserve / OCC, 2011) sets model risk management expectations widely applied in fair lending examinations (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau 2023)
United Kingdom FCA Consumer Duty (PS22/9) Firms must deliver good outcomes for all retail customers. Algorithmic pricing systems must demonstrate they do not systematically disadvantage identifiable groups (Financial Conduct Authority 2023)
European Union AI Act Annex III(5)(b) Credit scoring and credit risk assessment are classified as high-risk AI applications. Fairness, transparency, and human oversight requirements apply to deployed models (European Parliament and Council 2024)
Australia ASIC conduct obligations Consumer credit providers owe responsible lending and conduct duties under the National Consumer Credit Protection Act 2009. ASIC’s general conduct expectations apply to algorithmic decision-making in credit

The key regulatory distinction in lending, as in insurance, is between disparate treatment (explicitly using a protected attribute) and disparate impact (using neutral variables whose combined effect disadvantages a protected group). Most jurisdictions prohibit both, but the evidential standard and enforcement approach differ. The EU AI Act adds a governance layer on top: firms must document and test high-risk AI systems before deployment, not only in response to a complaint.

Mapping the four steps to lending

Step 1 · Define fairness

The fairness criteria in Step 1 apply directly to lending. \hat{Y} is typically a loan approval decision (binary) or an interest rate or credit limit (continuous).

The criteria most prominent in fair lending enforcement and academic work are:

  • Fairness through unawareness (FTU). The lending model does not use race or other protected attributes. This is the baseline in US fair lending compliance. As in insurance, it is insufficient when proxy variables carry protected-attribute information.
  • Conditional demographic parity (CDP). After controlling for legitimate credit risk factors, average rates or approval odds are equal across protected groups. This is the standard closest to what ECOA disparate-impact analysis asks for in practice.
  • No omitted-variable bias / controlling for the protected variable (CPV). The prediction each borrower receives does not depend on group membership, controlling for all risk factors. Fuster et al. (2022) use a framework closely related to this when evaluating whether FinTech models improve or worsen racial gaps.

What counts as a legitimate factor in lending is the analogue of the legitimate-factors question in insurance (Step 2). Repayment history, debt-to-income ratio, loan-to-value ratio, and credit utilisation are generally accepted. Geography (zip code, census tract) is contested: it carries credit signal but also redlining history. The list must be agreed with legal and compliance before any model is built.

Step 2 · Design fair pricing

The model designs from Step 2 (MU, MCDP, MC) carry over to lending with direct substitutions:

Insurance term Lending equivalent
Premium Interest rate or credit limit
Expected claim cost Probability of default (PD) or loss given default (LGD)
Claims history Repayment history, delinquency flag
Legitimate factors Credit risk factors accepted by the regulator
Community rating Uniform pricing regardless of risk

The core modelling question is the same: how much of the rate differential across protected groups reflects legitimate credit risk, and how much reflects proxy discrimination? MCDP separates these by retaining legitimate credit factors while debiasing other predictors. MC computes a discrimination-free rate by averaging the full model’s output over all protected-group values.

Bartlett et al. (2022) find that FinTech lenders charge minority borrowers approximately 7–8 basis points more on purchase mortgages and 3 basis points more on refinances after controlling for observable risk characteristics. Algorithmic lenders show smaller but still significant gaps. The CPV and MCDP designs offer a route to close those residual gaps while retaining risk sensitivity.

Step 3 · Assess impact

The welfare framework in Step 3 applies with one translation: welfare is now measured in interest rate differentials, approval rates, and credit access, not insurance premiums.

The structural tension identified in Step 3 — that price fairness and markup fairness pull in opposite directions — reappears in lending as a tension between rate fairness and profitability parity. A rule that equalises average rates across groups with different default risks requires the lender to cross-subsidise the higher-risk group, which affects return on equity and may reduce credit supply to the cross-subsidising group.

Participation effects are especially prominent in lending. When a fairness rule raises rates for lower-risk borrowers (to subsidise higher-risk borrowers in the same protected group), some lower-risk borrowers may leave the market or switch lenders, changing the composition of the remaining pool. The welfare calculation must account for this selection, as Step 3 discusses in the insurance context.

Step 4 · Audit the system

The audit protocol in Step 4 applies without modification. The same pre-commitment requirement, corrected inference (HC3), and equivalence testing framework apply to a lending audit. Response variable choices are:

  • Quoted interest rate or APR — the lending analogue of quoted premium
  • Approval indicator — a binary outcome, requiring a generalised linear model in the audit regression
  • Credit limit — a continuous outcome subject to the same analysis as a quoted price

The same pre-specification requirement applies. All design choices — fairness criterion, legitimate controls, tolerance bands, sample design — must be fixed before any lending data is examined.

CFPB and OCC fair lending examinations increasingly ask for exactly this kind of documented, pre-committed test protocol. The structure of Step 4 maps directly to what a model risk management (MRM) function should maintain for algorithmic lending systems under SR 11-7.
